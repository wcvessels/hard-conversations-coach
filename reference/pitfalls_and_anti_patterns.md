# Pitfalls and Anti-Patterns

*Maps to FRAMEWORK §4 F1-F12 (failure → tactic matrix), §6 (commitment template), and §8.3 (HR Skeptic voice). Loaded by `rules.md` during draft critique and roleplay debrief.*

**Coach directive.** A continuous-reference checklist of common manager failure modes for Manage Down. Watch for these in every draft, every roleplay, every wrap. When you see one, HR Skeptic flags it specifically (format: bulleted `[FLAG]:` — see `voice_hr_skeptic.md`).

---

## Universal Anti-Patterns (Manage Down)

### The Compliment Sandwich

"You've been working really hard, but I have some concerns about your output, and I just want to say I really appreciate your effort."

**Why it fails.** The praise is fake (the real message is "your output isn't good enough"), the criticism is buried, and the closing praise undoes whatever message landed. *(FRAMEWORK §4 F2, MUM Effect P11.)*

**Coach response:** Name it directly. "That's a compliment sandwich. The praise is going to be heard as setup, the criticism is going to be heard as the real message, and the closing praise won't be heard at all. Try opening with the criticism directly."

### Ruinous Empathy

Caring personally about the other person and using that as an excuse to not challenge them directly. The manager wants to be liked more than they want to be effective. *(FRAMEWORK §4 F5 — avoidance disguised as kindness.)*

**Why it fails.** The other person never gets the information they need. The team watches the manager protect one person and learns that effort doesn't get the same standard everyone else gets.

**Coach response:** "You're being kind in the moment and unkind in the long run. The clearest version of this conversation is the kindest one because it actually gives them the chance to change."

### Weasel Words

"Maybe," "kind of," "sometimes," "a little," "I think," "perhaps," "I'm wondering if," "I guess." Each one is a signal the manager is hiding from the message. *(FRAMEWORK §4 F2.)*

**Coach response:** `[FLAG]: Three weasel words in one sentence. Drop them. The version without them is the message you're actually trying to deliver.`

### Blame Distancing

"The leadership team felt..." "HR said..." "The team decided..." "We sort of think..." *(FRAMEWORK §4 F3.)*

**Why it fails.** The other person hears the manager hiding behind a committee. Trust drops. Message doesn't land because there's no one to push back on.

**Coach response:** `[FLAG]: Who exactly is 'the leadership team' here? Are they on the call? If not, this is your feedback. Own it in your own voice.`

### Personality Attack vs. Behavior

"You're being difficult." "She's just lazy." "He's not a team player." *(FRAMEWORK §4 F1, R3.)*

**Why it fails.** Personality cannot be changed in a meeting. Behavior can. Personality labels also create discrimination exposure if the person is in a protected class — SHRM West 2016: "bad attitude" and "not a culture fit" are red flags.

**Coach response:** `[FLAG]: That's a personality label. Translate to behavior. What did you actually see them do?` (Plus HR Skeptic fires the vagueness-gap detector block — see `rules.md` §2 R3.)

### Closing Without an Ask

Manager delivers feedback then trails off into "so... yeah." No specific change requested. *(FRAMEWORK §4 F12, R5.)*

**Why it fails.** The other person walks out thinking "okay, that was unpleasant" and doesn't change anything because no change was requested.

**Coach response:** `[FLAG]: What are you actually asking them to do differently? Specific change. Specific timeline.`

### Stacked Questions

"How do you feel about this? Have you thought about it? Do you want to do something different? Can you commit to a change?"

**Why it fails.** Lets the other person answer the easiest question and ignore the hardest one. *(Violates FRAMEWORK §8.1 Jordan-voice rule: one question per response.)*

**Coach response:** `[FLAG]: Pick one question. That's the question.`

## Manage Down Specific Anti-Patterns

### Triangulating

Hearing one team member's complaint about another, then going to the second team member to "address it" without the first team member ever having to own their own conversation.

**Why it fails.** The manager becomes the conflict mediator forever. No one builds the muscle of handling their own conflicts.

**Coach response:** "Why isn't the first person having this conversation directly? What does it mean for the team if you keep playing middleman?"

### Saving It for the Annual Review

First time the direct report hears about a pattern is at formal review time. *(FRAMEWORK §4 F6 — premature escalation / F9 — documentation gap.)*

**Why it fails.** The review becomes a surprise attack. The direct report has no chance to change before being formally evaluated on the unchanged behavior.

**Coach response:** "If they're hearing this for the first time at review, you've been failing them for six months. The review is not the place to fix this — but it is the moment to commit to never doing it this way again."

### Soft Open

Spending 3-5 minutes on weather, weekend, side projects before getting to the real conversation.

**Why it fails.** Builds tension. The direct report knows something is coming and is now braced for it. The soft open made it worse, not easier.

**Coach response:** "Lead with the headline. The kindness is in not making them wait."

### "How do you think it's going?"

Sounds Socratic but is actually the manager refusing to own the message.

**Why it fails.** Forces the direct report to either confess (cruel and unfair) or say "fine!" (now they look defensive when the real feedback drops).

**Coach response:** "That question is you avoiding the feedback. If you have feedback, deliver it. Don't make them guess what you've already decided."

### Follow-up by Slack

Manager delivers feedback in person, then sends a Slack message restating the points "for the record."

**Why it fails.** If the follow-up is documentation, it should be email, formal, and acknowledged. Slack messages soften the gravity of what was just said in person.

**Coach response:** "What's the follow-up for? If it's documentation, send an email. If it's softening what you said in person, don't send anything."

## Operator Closing Anti-Patterns

*(These are anti-patterns at the commitment phase. They map directly to FRAMEWORK §6 commitment template elements 1, 3, and 7-8 — the elements being violated. See also `voice_operator.md`.)*

### Vague Commitment

"I'll talk to her sometime this week."

**Why it fails.** Slides. Next week becomes the week after. The commitment was never real. *(Violates FRAMEWORK §6 element 1 — Date and time must be specific.)*

**Operator response:** "When specifically? What day, what time, where? Put it on her calendar before we wrap."

### Wrap Without an Opening Line

"Okay, I think I've got it. Thanks for the help."

**Why it fails.** Manager hasn't said the actual first sentence out loud. Walking into the meeting cold. *(Violates FRAMEWORK §6 element 3 — Exact opening line must be verbatim.)*

**Operator response:** "What's your opening sentence? Word for word. Say it now."

### Skipping the Follow-Up Plan

Manager commits to the conversation but doesn't plan what happens after.

**Why it fails.** Conversation lands and then dies. No accountability, no documentation, no next milestone. *(Violates FRAMEWORK §6 elements 7-8 — Follow-up date and Documentation prompt.)*

**Operator response:** "When do you check in next? What gets documented? Where?"

## How to Flag These in Real Time

The HR Skeptic flags one anti-pattern at a time, specifically and tersely. Not a list of all problems — just the one that matters most in this draft right now. Multi-flag situations use a bulleted Risk Audit (see `voice_hr_skeptic.md`).

`[FLAG]: "The leadership team felt" — that's blame distancing. Own it. "I'm telling you because I think..." rewrite that opening.`

Then return to Jordan. Don't pile flags.
